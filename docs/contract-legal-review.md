# Contract — legal review brief

**For:** the lawyer PESO recommended you consult, and for your own defense prep.
**Subject:** the CareLink employment contract (DOLE BK-1 form) generated at hire.

PESO Ormoc raised several points in the Aug 2026 review. This separates them
into **what the statute already answers** (implemented, cited) and **what
genuinely needs counsel** (not implemented, flagged).

Governing law: **RA 10361**, the Domestic Workers Act / Batas Kasambahay (2013),
and its IRR. Supporting: PD 851 (13th month pay), RA 8792 (E-Commerce Act) for
electronic signatures. 

---

## Plain-language guide to RA 10361 (Sections 1–45)

This is a simplified implementation reference, not legal advice or a substitute
for the Act, its Implementing Rules and Regulations (IRR), current wage orders,
or advice from Philippine labor counsel. Read it together with those primary
sources. Do not use the original 2013 peso amounts or contribution thresholds
as today's amounts without checking current issuances.

### Article I — General rules and who is covered (Sections 1–4)

- **Section 1 — Name:** The law is the Domestic Workers Act, also called the
  Batas Kasambahay.
- **Section 2 — Purpose:** The State protects domestic workers' labor rights,
  dignity, safety, fair work, social protection, and freedom from child labor,
  forced labor, discrimination, abuse, and trafficking, and promotes
  gender-sensitive protections.
- **Section 3 — Coverage:** It applies to domestic workers employed and working
  in the Philippines.
- **Section 4 — Key terms:** Domestic work is work performed in or for a
  household. A kasambahay is a person doing that work in an employment
  relationship, including a general househelper, yaya/nursemaid, cook,
  gardener, or laundry worker. The Act excludes a person who performs domestic
  work **only occasionally or sporadically and not on an occupational basis**.
  Both parts of that exclusion matter. A one-time task is not automatically
  outside the Act, and duration by itself does not settle coverage. The Act
  also excludes children in a foster-family arrangement when the statutory
  education/allowance conditions apply. An employer is the person who engages
  and controls the worker's services and is party to the employment contract.

### Article II — Rights and protections (Sections 5–10)

- **Section 5 — Respectful treatment:** No abuse, physical violence,
  harassment, or degrading treatment.
- **Section 6 — Basic necessities and care:** Provide at least three adequate
  meals a day and safe, humane sleeping arrangements. Provide appropriate
  rest and assistance for illness or injury sustained during service. These
  necessities cannot be withheld as punishment.
- **Section 7 — Privacy:** Respect the worker's privacy, communications, and
  personal belongings.
- **Section 8 — Communication:** Allow outside communication during free time
  and during work time in an emergency. The worker ordinarily pays charges
  for using the employer's phone unless the employer waives them.
- **Section 9 — Education and training:** Give the worker an opportunity to
  complete basic education and, as practicable, pursue further education or
  vocational training; adjust the schedule where practicable.
- **Section 10 — Household confidentiality:** Household information is
  confidential during and after employment, subject to the Act's exception
  for cases involving specified crimes by the employer or household members.

### Article III — Hiring and pre-employment (Sections 11–18)

- **Section 11 — Written employment contract:** Employer and worker must
  execute the contract **before service begins**, in a language both
  understand, and the worker must receive a signed copy. It must state duties,
  employment period, compensation, lawful deductions, hours and additional
  pay, rest days and leaves, board/lodging/medical care, deployment-expense
  arrangements, loans, termination terms, and other lawful agreed conditions.
  DOLE provides a model contract.
- **Section 12 — Possible pre-employment documents:** An employer may require
  a health certificate, barangay/police/NBI clearances, and proof of age.
  The employer or agency pays the cost. These are standard requirements when
  hiring is facilitated by a private employment agency (PEA).
- **Section 13 — Recruitment fees:** A PEA or third party may not charge the
  worker a share of recruitment or finder's fees.
- **Section 14 — Loss/damage deposits:** An employer may not require deposits
  from the worker to cover household property or equipment loss/damage.
- **Section 15 — Debt bondage:** Using service as an unclear or unreasonable
  debt payment arrangement is prohibited.
- **Section 16 — Minimum age:** A person below 15 may not be employed as a
  domestic worker. Workers aged 15–17 are working children and receive the
  Act's protections plus the relevant child-protection rules. A platform
  should apply stricter safeguarding and counsel-reviewed age verification.
- **Section 17 — Barangay registry:** Employers must register their
  kasambahay with the domestic-worker registry in the barangay where the
  employer lives, following the applicable process.
- **Section 18 — Skills development:** DOLE, through TESDA, facilitates
  domestic-worker training, assessment, and certification.

### Article IV — Work terms, wages, rest, and benefits (Sections 19–31)

- **Section 19 — Health and safety:** The employer must safeguard the
  worker's health and safety, considering the nature of domestic work.
- **Section 20 — Daily rest:** The worker is entitled to an aggregate of at
  least eight hours of rest in each day. This is a rest entitlement, not a
  statutory eight-hour workday.
- **Section 21 — Weekly rest:** At least 24 consecutive hours of weekly rest.
  The schedule is agreed in writing, respecting a religious preference.
  The Act permits agreed arrangements such as offsetting absence, paid
  waiver of a particular rest day, or accumulating no more than five rest
  days.
- **Section 22 — Non-household enterprise work:** A domestic worker assigned
  to commercial, industrial, or agricultural enterprise work must receive
  at least the applicable minimum wage for that kind of worker.
- **Section 23 — Temporary work for another household:** This requires the
  worker's agreement. The original employer remains responsible for
  liabilities, the worker must receive additional pay of at least the
  applicable minimum wage rate for a domestic worker, and the original
  employer may not charge the other household for the worker's service.
- **Section 24 — Minimum wage:** Regional wage boards review and set domestic
  worker rates. The Act's original 2013 figures are historical baselines, not
  a reliable statement of today's floor. Check the current wage order for
  the region and municipality before displaying or enforcing an amount.
- **Section 25 — Payment and 13th-month pay:** Pay wages on time, directly to
  the worker, in cash at least monthly. Deductions are limited to those
  allowed by law unless the worker gives written consent. The worker is
  entitled to 13th-month pay under PD 851. **This is Section 25**, not
  Section 27.
- **Section 26 — Payslips:** Provide a payslip each payday showing cash paid
  and deductions; keep copies for three years.
- **Section 27 — Control of wages:** The employer may not force the worker
  to buy goods or services or otherwise control how wages are spent.
- **Section 28 — Withholding wages:** Wages generally may not be withheld.
  The Act states a narrow consequence for a worker leaving without
  justifiable reason, including possible forfeiture of unpaid salary up to
  15 days; do not implement deductions, forfeitures, or debt recovery
  automatically without counsel-reviewed criteria.
- **Section 29 — Service incentive leave (SIL):** After one year of service,
  five paid leave days per year. The Act says unused days do not carry over
  and are not convertible to cash.
- **Section 30 — Social protection:** After at least one month of service,
  coverage includes SSS, PhilHealth, and Pag-IBIG, under the relevant laws.
  The employer shoulders contributions subject to the worker's lawful
  proportionate share under applicable rules. Contribution splits and
  thresholds can change; do not hard-code the Act's original peso threshold.
- **Section 31 — Rescue:** Local social-welfare officers/DSWD, coordinating
  with barangay officials, provide rescue and rehabilitation for abused or
  exploited domestic workers.

### Article V — Ending employment (Sections 32–35)

- **Section 32 — Termination framework:** Before a fixed term expires,
  neither party may end it except on the statutory grounds in Sections 33
  or 34. If the duration is undetermined by agreement and by the nature of
  the service, either party may give five days' notice. The parties may also
  agree in writing to end the contract early. An unjustly dismissed worker
  is owed earned compensation plus 15 days' work as indemnity. If a worker
  leaves without justifiable reason, the Act describes forfeiture of up to
  15 days' unpaid salary and, under its conditions, recovery of deployment
  expenses when service ends within six months. These are legal remedies,
  not a platform-imposed "payment in lieu of notice".
- **Section 33 — Worker-initiated termination:** The worker may end service
  for verbal/emotional or physical abuse, a crime against the worker,
  employer violation of the contract/law, a disease prejudicial to either
  party or the household, or analogous causes.
- **Section 34 — Employer-initiated termination:** Grounds include
  misconduct/willful disobedience of a lawful work order, gross or habitual
  neglect/inefficiency, fraud or willful breach of trust, a crime against
  the employer or immediate family, worker violation of the contract/law,
  a prejudicial disease, or analogous causes.
- **Section 35 — Work certificate:** On request, the employer must issue a
  certificate stating the nature, duration, and performance of service
  within five days.

### Article VI — Private employment agencies (Section 36)

DOLE licenses and regulates PEAs. A PEA is jointly and severally liable with
the employer for wages and employment benefits due. It must not charge the
worker recruitment fees, must ensure lawful contract terms and benefits,
brief both parties before employment, keep contracts, assist with grievances,
and cooperate in rescue operations.

### Article VII — Disputes (Section 37)

Labor-related disputes go to the DOLE Regional Office with jurisdiction over
the workplace, which must attempt conciliation and mediation before deciding.
This does not prevent appropriate civil or criminal actions. Ordinary crimes
are handled by the regular courts. CareLink must not describe a barangay or
PESO step as a mandatory legal prerequisite unless counsel confirms it.

### Article VIII — Government programs (Sections 38–39)

DOLE and partner agencies must provide continuing information about the Act.
The date of presidential approval is designated Araw ng mga Kasambahay.

### Article IX — Penalties and transition (Sections 40–42)

- **Section 40 — Penalty:** Violations declared unlawful under the Act may
  carry a ₱10,000–₱40,000 fine, without preventing other civil or criminal
  actions.
- **Section 41 — Transition/non-diminution:** Existing arrangements had to
  be brought up to the Act's minimums; wage adjustments take effect under
  the applicable wage-board issuance. Existing better benefits must not be
  reduced or replaced by the statutory minimums.
- **Section 42 — IRR:** Directs the relevant agencies to issue implementing
  rules. The IRR supplies operational detail and should be checked alongside
  the Act.

### Article X — Final rules (Sections 43–45)

- **Section 43 — Severability:** If a provision is invalidated, unaffected
  provisions remain effective.
- **Section 44 — Repeal:** Inconsistent laws and the old Labor Code
  househelper provisions are repealed or modified.
- **Section 45 — Effectivity:** The Act took effect 15 days after the
  required publication.

## Part 1 — Answered by RA 10361, now in the contract

### 1.1 Thirteenth month pay — ADDED
PESO said 13th month pay belongs in mandatory benefits. Correct.

> **Sec. 25.** *Payment of Wages.* — "The domestic worker is entitled to a
> thirteenth month pay as provided for by law."

Eligibility and computation are governed by PD 851 and its rules, including
the applicable service requirement. The benefit is listed **unconditionally
and first** in the contract clause; it is not an employer-selected job-post
toggle.
Social-insurance coverage is also mandatory when eligibility applies; contribution
shares must follow current SSS, PhilHealth, and Pag-IBIG rules, not a stale
hard-coded threshold.

**Also added:** service incentive leave — five days with pay after one year
(Sec. 29). It was absent for the same reason.

### 1.2 Working hours — REWRITTEN
PESO said: not more than 12 hours, 8 hours maximum normal, overtime must be paid.

What the statute actually guarantees:

> **Sec. 20.** *Daily Rest Period.* — The domestic worker shall be entitled to an
> aggregate daily rest period of eight (8) hours per day.
>
> **Sec. 21.** *Weekly Rest Period.* — ...at least twenty-four (24) consecutive
> hours of rest in a week.

Note what that does **not** say: RA 10361 does not itself impose an 8-hour
working day or a 12-hour maximum workday. It guarantees rest, not those work
limits. The contract may state an 8-hour normal schedule and a 12-hour ceiling
as additional CareLink contract protections, but must not attribute those
figures to Sections 20–21.

The clause stipulates normal work not exceeding 8 hours per day, compensated
overtime requiring the helper's consent, a 12-hour daily ceiling, and the
statutory 8-hour daily and 24-hour weekly rest. The 8-hour work schedule and
12-hour ceiling are **contract terms**, not statutory maximum hours. Confirm
their wording and overtime treatment with counsel.

In the hire form, a fixed schedule's displayed work-hour estimate subtracts a
default one-hour lunch break, and the API applies the 12-hour check to that
estimate. The BK-1 prints the selected start/end window but does not separately
specify the meal-break arrangement. The form asks the parties to agree on the
actual break and record any different arrangement under Other terms; confirm
this treatment with counsel before relying on it.

### 1.3 Grounds for termination — ADDED, VERBATIM
PESO said they did not know what grounds justify immediate termination. The
statute lists them, so the contract now reproduces both sections rather than
paraphrasing.

**Sec. 33 — the HELPER may end service for:** verbal or emotional abuse;
inhuman treatment including physical abuse; commission of a crime or offense
against the helper; violation by the employer of the contract terms or the law;
any disease prejudicial to the health of either party or the household; other
analogous causes.

**Sec. 34 — the EMPLOYER may end service for:** misconduct or willful
disobedience of a lawful work-related order; gross or habitual neglect or
inefficiency; fraud or willful breach of trust; commission of a crime or offense
against the employer or an immediate family member; violation by the helper of
the contract terms or the law; any disease prejudicial to health; other
analogous causes.

### 1.4 A wrong citation, now corrected — IMPORTANT

The old contract said:

> *"Either party may terminate this agreement with at least 30 days written
> notice or payment in lieu thereof (₱7,000.00), as provided under RA 10361."*

**That was wrong on two counts, and it was attributed to the statute**, which
makes it worse than saying nothing — it reads as authoritative.

| Old clause said | RA 10361 Sec. 32 actually says |
|---|---|
| 30 days written notice | **5 days** notice — and only where the duration of service is undetermined |
| "payment in lieu" of notice | No such thing. There is an **indemnity of 15 days' work** where the helper is unjustly dismissed |
| — | Where the helper leaves without justifiable cause, up to **15 days' unpaid salary** is forfeited |
| — | Before the term expires, neither party may terminate **except on Sec. 33/34 grounds** |

The one-month notice rule appears in some Labor Code contexts; it is not the
notice rule in RA 10361 Section 32. A kasambahay is governed by RA 10361 for
these termination terms. The clause now states Section 32 correctly.

> **Ask counsel to confirm this reading**, and specifically whether a contract
> may lawfully stipulate a *longer* notice period than the statutory five days
> where that favours the worker.

## Part 2 — Product policies and questions for counsel

### 2.1 Duration, short engagements, and one-time tasks

RA 10361 does not set a minimum contract length. Section 4's exclusion is
limited to domestic work performed **both** only occasionally or sporadically
**and** not on an occupational basis. The label "one-time", a short duration,
or a single client does not independently decide whether the Act applies. The
actual facts and relationship matter; this needs Philippine labor counsel where
classification is uncertain.

**Current CareLink behavior:**

- The employer job form lets the employer choose recurring employment or a
  one-time task. Selecting one-time is blocked in
  [`EngagementTypeSelector.tsx`](../frontend/components/parent/jobs/EngagementTypeSelector.tsx)
  and authoritatively rejected by [`post_job.php`](../backend/parent/post_job.php).
  That is a **product-support limitation**, not a legal determination that
  every one-time task is outside RA 10361. The UI and API say so explicitly.
- A fixed-term contract generated through CareLink must cover at least **30
  inclusive calendar days**. This CareLink policy is enforced in the hire UI
  and centrally in
  [`contract_generator.inc.php`](../backend/contracts/contract_generator.inc.php)
  for contract creation and edits. The backend also requires an agreed start
  date, disallows an end date for indefinite contracts, and verifies that a
  fixed-term duration label matches the actual dates. CareLink now also caps
  fixed-term contracts at **2 calendar years**; longer employment uses
  Indefinite. Both duration limits are CareLink product policies, not statutory
  thresholds. The hire form and server cap each duration unit to that range
  (30–731 days depending on the start date, 5–104 weeks, 1–24 months, or 1–2
  years), then verify the actual calendar dates. Indefinite contracts require a
  start date but have no end date. The 30-day floor is not in RA 10361, does not
  determine whether someone is a kasambahay, and may prevent the platform from
  handling a short engagement that is legally employment. The 2-year ceiling
  likewise should not be represented as a legal limit. Both policies should be
  reviewed with counsel. These rules apply when proposing or editing a
  contract; regenerating a PDF for an already finalized or legacy agreement
  does not retroactively change that agreement.
- Salary amounts in job posts and contracts are monthly; the selected payment
  schedule controls when that amount is paid, not the salary basis. The hire
  form uses the same payout breakdown as the job-post form: semi-monthly is
  split exactly, while daily and weekly figures are estimates (26 working days
  and about 4.33 weeks per month, respectively). Custom schedules require the
  parties to enter their installment timing and amounts. The contract now
  states the monthly salary and payment schedule separately, and salary-related
  calculations do not multiply the monthly amount by a daily or weekly cadence.
  This product behavior is implemented in
  [`salary.ts`](../frontend/lib/salary.ts) and
  [`contract_generator.inc.php`](../backend/contracts/contract_generator.inc.php).
- Contract creation currently requires an existing job application and job
  post: [`hire_helper.php`](../backend/parent/hire_helper.php) validates both,
  and the shared generator joins the application to its job post. There is no
  current standalone direct-hire/no-post contract flow.

**Immediate start versus immediate/direct hire:**

- If "immediate" means a same-day start, Section 11 requires the employment
  contract to be executed before service starts. A defensible same-day flow
  must give both parties time to review and agree before any work begins; it
  must not backdate or treat a draft as consent. Counsel should review what
  counts as execution for CareLink's electronic-signature process.
- If "immediate hire" means hiring a helper without first posting a job, that
  is not supported by the current contract endpoint. A future direct-hire
  path should create a private employment opportunity/record, capture the
  terms and worker's acceptance, and reuse the same eligibility, wage, date,
  and contract-signing guards. It should not skip the contract flow.

**Can CareLink support one-time work? Potentially, but not as a checkbox that
waives employment protections.** There are two different product cases:

1. **Short or single-scheduled domestic work that is actually occupational
   employment:** use the employment path and apply the law based on the real
   relationship. The current 30-day platform floor would need a specific
   policy decision if CareLink intends to support covered employment shorter
   than 30 days; the law itself does not supply that floor.
2. **A genuinely occasional, sporadic, non-occupational task:** if counsel
   confirms it falls outside the employment relationship/RA 10361, it could
   use a separate task-booking flow and agreement rather than a kasambahay
   employment contract. That flow still needs reviewed rules for scope,
   price/payment, cancellation, safety, liability, complaints/disputes,
   identity, and prohibited practices. Other laws may still apply.

Until counsel and the product owner approve which cases CareLink will support,
keep standalone task bookings blocked—but never explain the block as a legal
finding. The next legal questions are: how to identify occupational basis in
borderline cases; whether/how Section 11 applies to very short covered
employment; what other legal rules govern non-employment task bookings; and
whether a same-day contract can be executed with the current e-signature flow.

### 2.2 Overtime premium rate
The contract requires overtime to be compensated at "the agreed overtime rate".
RA 10361 does not set a premium percentage for kasambahay the way the Labor Code
does (25% ordinary day, 30% rest day/holiday).

Question: does the Labor Code overtime premium apply to domestic workers by
analogy or by IRR, or is the rate purely a matter of agreement? Right now the
contract leaves it to the parties, which is defensible but may under-protect.

### 2.3 Night work
Sec. 20's eight-hour aggregate rest does not distinguish day from night work.
Ask whether the Labor Code night-shift differential (Art. 86) reaches kasambahay.

### 2.4 Disputes and escalation
Section 37 directs labor-related disputes to the DOLE Regional Office with
jurisdiction over the workplace and requires conciliation/mediation before a
decision. Civil or criminal actions remain possible where appropriate.
CareLink's Barangay/PESO support channel can help with intake and referrals,
but it must not be described as a mandatory legal prerequisite to a DOLE
complaint unless counsel confirms the applicable process.

## Part 3 — Electronic signature

PESO asked that the signature be secure. It now is, within what a system with no
certificate authority can honestly claim.

**Before:** signing wrote one timestamp — `employer_signed_at` /
`helper_signed_at`. That records that a database row changed. It proves nothing
about who signed, what they signed, or whether the document changed afterwards.

**Now** (`shared/contract_signatures_table.php`), each signature records:

| Field | What it establishes |
|---|---|
| `document_hash` | SHA-256 of the contract as it stood at signing — re-hash later and any alteration shows |
| `signature_seal` | HMAC over signer + role + document hash + timestamp, keyed with the server secret — a row edited in the database no longer verifies |
| `auth_method` | how identity was proven (password re-entry at signing) |
| `ip_address`, `user_agent`, `consent_text` | the circumstances a dispute turns on |

Mapped to **RA 8792** and its IRR, which treat an electronic signature as
reliable when it is (a) uniquely linked to the signatory, (b) capable of
identifying them, (c) under their sole control, and (d) linked to the data so
that any change is detectable:

- (a) and (b) — signer id bound into the sealed record
- (c) — password re-entry at the moment of signing (`v1/auth/verify_password.php`)
- (d) — the document hash

Verified behaviour (tested):

```
same document        -> seal valid, document matches
altered document     -> seal valid, document MISMATCH   <- alteration detected
tampered database row-> seal INVALID                    <- tampering detected
```

### What this is NOT — say this plainly
This is a **reliable electronic signature with an audit trail**, not a *digital
signature* in the PKI sense. There is no certificate authority and the signer
holds no private key, so it cannot claim the legal presumption that attaches to
a digitally signed document under RA 8792 Sec. 8. If a panel or counsel asks
whether this would hold up in court, the honest answer is: it is strong
evidence of assent and of document integrity, and it is not a notarised or
PKI-backed signature.

**Ask counsel:** is this sufficient for a BK-1 employment contract, or does DOLE
expect a wet signature or a notarised copy on file for kasambahay contracts?

## Summary for the defense

If asked "is your contract legally sound?":

> The contract is based on the DOLE model form. Its stated benefits and
> termination grounds are mapped to RA 10361 with section numbers cited. The
> contract's 8-hour normal schedule and 12-hour ceiling are additional CareLink
> contract terms; the statute itself guarantees rest periods rather than those
> work-hour limits. We corrected a clause that misattributed a 30-day notice
> period to the Act: five days' notice applies when service duration is
> undetermined, and a 15-day indemnity applies to unjust dismissal. The
> electronic signing flow records identity and document-integrity evidence,
> but it is not a PKI digital signature. The Act's occasional-work exclusion
> requires both occasional/sporadic work and a non-occupational basis.
> CareLink's 30-day fixed-term floor is a product rule, not a statutory minimum
> or coverage test. This is an implementation summary—not a legal opinion—and
> the documented questions should be reviewed with Philippine labor counsel.
